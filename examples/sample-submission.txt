When the gatekeepers broke the wax seal on the cedar tablets, the hall did not fall silent so much as draw inward, like a lung before drowning. Ilen stood beneath the soot-dark rafters and watched the old law lifted into torchlight. The letters had once governed kings. Now they trembled in the hands of clerks.

No one looked at him. That was the first sentence of exile.

The queen remained upon the basalt dais, narrow as a blade in mourning blue. Around her throat hung the river-token of the ancestor bride, a stone cut from the ford where, legend claimed, the first covenant had been sworn between city and flood. In childhood Ilen had thought the token a charm against ruin. Tonight it seemed only a weight.

He had ridden three days from the eastern watchtowers to tell them the canals were failing. The snowmelt came wrong this year, thin and early, and the farmers had already begun to fight with spades in the dry beds. He had expected argument, delay, perhaps even contempt. He had not expected the tablets.

The chief clerk read the forbidden clause in a voice too careful to be brave: when the river recedes beyond the black stair for three mornings, the blood of the river-house must answer for the breach.

At that, the queen's fingers tightened once upon the arm of her seat. It was the only movement she allowed herself. The courtiers shifted like reeds in a foul current. Still no one met Ilen's eyes. They did not need to. The house named in the law had only one living child.

He thought then of the eastern tower, of its cracked cistern and the red kite that nested in the watchfire cage. He thought of ordinary things because the sacred ones had all become instruments.

"The stair was marked by traitors," he said. "The river has changed its course twice since this law was cut. If we bind ourselves to dead measurements, we choose famine."

The words reached the far walls and died there. The queen studied him as though memorizing a face already gone from the world.

"If I break covenant," she said at last, "the valley lords will call the city faithless. If I keep it, I lose my son to a river that no longer remembers its own name."

She rose then, and every torch in the hall seemed to lean toward her. The river-token flashed once against her throat. With a motion almost gentle, she drew the cord over her head and held the stone in her palm.

"Let the record show," she said, "that the covenant breaks here."

The clerk blanched. A murmur ran through the chamber like the first crack in winter ice.

Ilen understood too late. To break the covenant publicly was to unmake the throne's holiness. The valley lords would come. The city would divide. The old peace, brittle and burdensome as it had been, would not survive the season.

His mother set the stone upon the tablets.

"Send riders east," she said. "Open the granaries. Burn the stair."

The token rested on the ancient law like a second seal, not preserving but ending. In that moment Ilen loved her with the helplessness one reserves for the doomed, and knew that by dawn the kingdom would begin to devour the mercy that had saved him.
